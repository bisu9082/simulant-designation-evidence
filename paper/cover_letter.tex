We submit \textit{What Chemical Warfare Agent Simulants Are Validated For, and What Descriptor Comparisons Can Decide} for consideration as
an Article.

Analytical chemistry for chemical warfare agents is almost never done with the
agents. Arrays, sorbents and sampling protocols are characterised on simulants,
so the choice of simulant sets a ceiling on what a published detection result
can be taken to mean. That choice is made by a convention, and the convention is
stated plainly enough to test. Webb, Coulon and Temple (\textit{J.\ Hazard.\
Mater.} \textbf{492}, 138021, 2025) give the criterion:
align hydrophobicity, volatility and solubility with the agent, and the compounds
so chosen present minimal toxicity risk. Both halves are testable against
existing measurement. We tested them, and we first asked a prior question the
criterion presupposes: which document designates these compounds, and for
which measurement. The answer to that question is the paper's first result,
and it decides how much the descriptor comparison can be asked to carry.

\textbf{In the record we could reach, no incumbent simulant carries a public
designation that validates a detection response against an agent's.} We looked for the instrument that
designates them, and we say at the outset where that search falls short: the US
Army procedure titled \emph{Simulant Selection for Laboratory, Chamber, and
Field Testing} is not public, and we could not obtain it. We know its title and
its role only because a public procedure defers simulant selection to it. That
same public procedure states a detection-relevant criterion, which is evidence
against the strong form of our claim, and we report it in the paper rather than
leaving a reviewer to find it. DMMP was identified as a filtration-breakthrough test simulant in a NIOSH
respirator programme, in a document that also declines to recommend any
simulant, and a second designation names it for fabric penetration against VX.
% 2026-11-12. 특허 본문을 직독해 철회를 되돌렸다. 2026-11-03 의 '원문
% 미확인' 은 시도 실패의 기록이었고 문서에 대한 사실이 아니었다.
DPGME is designated in a US Army patent for a chemical agent simulant training
composition, which names its agent classes, liquid G-agent including VX and
H-agent. What the patent requires is detectability, not resemblance: its first
claim asks only that the mixture be detectable by a vapour detection apparatus,
and no comparison with an agent's response is given. For dichloromethane we found no designating document
at all; it is absent from the NIOSH document and from the Man-in-Simulant
Test programme. The record separates into three grades, which the manuscript
tabulates: a compound named for a stated measurement, a compound recorded only
as having been used, and a compound with no document. The clearest designation
belongs to none of the three: methyl salicylate is the Army's Man-in-Simulant
Test simulant and the subject of a published ASTM test method, which fixes the
measurement as vapour penetration of a protective ensemble. The National
Academies assessment of that programme nonetheless records that its selection
rested on historical use and that the assumptions behind it have not been
tested. A simulant is validated against a
measurement, not against an agent, and that validation does not travel with the
compound into sensor development.

\textbf{On a panel that contains the compounds the field itself uses as
simulants, none of eleven agents has a valid match to an incumbent simulant.}
For eleven authentic agents we computed RDKit structural and GFN2-xTB electronic
descriptors and asked, by exact permutation over every possible designation,
whether dimethyl methylphosphonate, dichloromethane and dipropylene glycol
monomethyl ether sit closer to the agents than compounds drawn at random. Our
primary panel of thirty-five compounds includes five further compounds the
sensor, sorbent and decontamination literature uses as simulants (DFP, DCP,
DCNP, CEES, methyl salicylate). On it, not one agent is matched, and not one of
eighteen tests in three descriptor spaces under six distance statistics reaches
nominal significance. The mechanism is not a loss of fidelity. The incumbent compounds have not become
less faithful. Within the larger panel DMMP still sits 1.18 typical
nearest-neighbour separations from sarin; what the panel supplies is
diisopropyl fluorophosphate at 1.06 from sarin, 1.00 from soman and 1.11 from
cyclosarin, and 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide at 0.53 from sulfur mustard against
dichloromethane's 1.59. Resampling the 41 descriptors 1000 times leaves this
intact: the incumbent three match a median of zero agents (2.5th to 97.5th
percentiles zero to three) against five (four to six) for the eight designated
candidates
examined here, and
the difference is positive in every replicate. A designation defended by a
comparison that excludes the alternatives is not defended.

\textbf{The twenty-eight-compound panel that omits those five is reported in
full, because the contrast is the result.} On it, three agents are matched, DMMP
supplies all three, and the smallest nominal $p$ is 0.025. How that is corrected
decides whether it means anything, so we report six schemes rather than one:
under Benjamini-Hochberg across the eighteen tests, across six within each
space, at the effective number of independent tests estimated from the
correlation of the permutation nulls, or under the Westfall-Young min-$P$
procedure, which handles the dependence among the eighteen exactly because they
share one enumeration of designations, nothing reaches 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg
over six within each space, at 0.077, is the closest any correction brings it;
the Westfall-Young value is 0.079). Had the statistic
been specified in advance, it would survive at $q=0.042$. We did not specify one.
That contrast is the paper's argument made with its own data. The observed value
also sits at the 22.4th percentile of 2000 arbitrary three-descriptor subspaces
of the same descriptor block.

\textbf{One axis behaves differently, and it is the one the criterion names.}
Volatility is a single measured quantity rather than a descriptor set, so the
analyst choices above do not arise on it. We specified two tests in advance of
it, outside the eighteen-test families. The first was reported in the form we
specified: over all 680 designations on the twenty-eight-compound panel the
incumbent set ranks fourteenth on the pre-specified statistic, a one-sided $p$
of 0.0206 on that enumeration. The support is in aggregate distance only. Scored by valid
matches the same designation ranks 316th of 680, the axis yields one match, and
that match pairs DPGME with soman across a gap smaller than the mismatch between
the temperatures at which the two vapour pressures were measured.

The second test was not reported in the form we specified, and we would rather
say so here than have you find it. The specified form combined every evaluable
cell in one Fisher step and returned $p = 0.0809$. We replaced it the same day
with a per-channel form, under which the reflectance channel reaches
$q = 0.0430$, and the reason for replacing it is sound: two channels of one
plate are not independent. But we chose that form after seeing what the
specified one returned, which is the circumstance a prespecification exists to
exclude, and it is the circumstance this paper is about. The manuscript now
reports the specified form as the result of that test and the per-channel form
as a declared post hoc sensitivity with no exemption from correction. The
family boundary on that axis is also one we drew ourselves, at two optical
channels rather than the twelve cells used above, and the direction of that
choice favours our own result.

\textbf{Taken together, we cannot establish that the incumbent simulants
resemble the agents in the descriptor spaces, and we cannot establish that they
do not.} Three things are
open to the analyst: the compound panel, the descriptor space, and the distance
statistic. We fixed all three in every combination we could compute, which is
eighteen tests on each of four panel and designation configurations, and we
report the whole table rather than a cell from it.

\textbf{One widely used descriptor turned out not to be a coordinate.} The Parr
electrophilicity index $\omega = \mu^{2}/2\eta$ is an exact function of the two
frontier orbital energies normally reported beside it; across our panel the
deviation from the definition reaches at most $1.3\times10^{-5}$~eV on the
primary panel and $7.1\times10^{-15}$~eV on the twenty-eight-compound one. A correlation
filter does not catch this, because $r(E_\mathrm{LUMO}, \omega) = -0.899$. We
removed it, which cost the electronic space a dimension it never had, and we
state the general form of the point in the Discussion because any descriptor set
reporting $E_\mathrm{HOMO}$, $E_\mathrm{LUMO}$, the gap, $\mu$, $\eta$ and
$\omega$ as six quantities has two.

\textbf{The measured array agrees that the question is unresolved, and corrects
us on one point.} Twenty-nine-component colorimetric response for twelve of the
compounds, tested by Mantel permutation over compounds rather than over the
pairs they generate, gives three nominally significant cells out of twelve that
do not agree on a descriptor space, none of which survives correction across
the twelve (smallest $q$ = 0.117). Those exposures were acetonitrile solutions
at matched molarity rather than vapour, which bounds what any of it establishes
about a vapour-phase response, and we say so in the paper. Three artifacts of this kind of array analysis
are reported alongside, because our own first version contained all three:
we read the between-compound mean cosine against an algebraic bound of
$-1/(n-1)$, which the renormalisation step in our own processing removes, and
we now test the premise against a label-permutation null instead; six dyes that do
not fluoresce under ultraviolet illumination returned one background reading six
times, which inflated the two strongest cells until we collapsed them; and the
measured agent nearest to DMMP in the ultraviolet channel is tabun, not sarin,
although tabun fails our own descriptor matching criterion. We also state
there what the three nominal cells cannot carry: all three fall in the plate
set that holds no simulant, so they compare agents with agents, while the six
cells that bear on the premise are null and the design could only have
registered a near-deterministic association. We would rather submit that
sentence than a stronger one we cannot support.

\textbf{The two incumbents differ in safety evidence, not only in hazard.}
Dichloromethane has a regulator-derived acute inhalation value and it is
favourable on that axis, sitting at the 96th percentile of the 207-compound pool
that carries one. The manuscript is explicit that this is the acute axis alone,
and that it says nothing about the chronic and carcinogenic classification
now displacing dichloromethane from solvent inventories. DMMP has no value
at all, because EPA declined to derive an inhalation reference concentration for
want of data. Reporting the two as one category of low-toxicity simulant
obscures that difference, which matters because DMMP is also the compound against
which decontamination efficiency is quantified.

\textbf{A model trained on the regulatory inventory cannot fill the gap, and the
failure is instructive.} A random forest on 230 AEGL chemicals reaches
$R^{2}=0.458$ with confidence intervals that do not overlap its baseline. Applied
to the twenty military agents held out of training, it predicts every one of them
safer than measured, by factors of 2.5 to 2600, and certifies fourteen of the
twenty as inside its applicability domain. We checked the obvious explanation and
it is wrong: thirteen training compounds carry an acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting
pharmacophore, identified by substructure and listed in the paper, and one of
them is more acutely toxic than sarin. The model fails because it regresses both
tails toward the mean, a bias defined in the response space that an input-space
applicability domain cannot see.

\textbf{The two axes are not monotonically associated.} Across 207 compounds
carrying both measured axes, Spearman $r = +0.062$ (95\% CI $-0.077$ to
$+0.199$). Eight
compounds combine top-quartile fidelity with measured acute inhalation safety
within an order of magnitude of dichloromethane, all eight clear a structural
filter for agent-class motifs, and all eight are more faithful than
dichloromethane itself. We propose none of them as a replacement, and the reason
is the paper's own argument: a recommendation resting on one panel, one space and
two axes would repeat the error we are reporting.

\textbf{On scope and length.} The paper reports no new measurement, and the
colorimetric response against which it tests one premise was published in
earlier work from this laboratory; what is new is theoretical and documentary,
which is the kind of Article your guidelines describe when they say an Article
may be theoretical. The new material is the graded record of what each
designating document actually validates, an exhaustive enumeration of every
way three non-agents could have been designated on each panel, with the larger
designation null and the subspace controls sampled at 20000 and 2000 draws
rather than enumerated, a re-parse of the regulatory
acute-inhalation table at cell level, and a held-out test of whether a model
can fill the toxicity gap. The question is what a measurement on a
surrogate is evidence for, which is a question about analytical validation
rather than about any one instrument, and the compounds at issue are the ones
this journal's chemical warfare agent sensing papers are characterised on.
On length we state our position under both of the conversions your author
guidelines carry. Against the word count of the current page, 10,000 words for
an Article, the manuscript stands at 7,752 words of text, abstract, title block
and references, or 8,252 with its one double-column figure converted at 500
words. Against the earlier conversion of eight journal pages at 1,000 words per
page it stands at 8.25 pages, a quarter page over; we ask your indulgence for
that quarter page and will cut it on request. Reaching even that figure meant
moving the descriptor-geometry detail, the toxicity axis, the dual-use audit and
five of the six figures to the Supporting Information, and we have kept in the
main text the
disclosures a reader needs in order to weigh the claims: which form of each
test was specified in advance, where we drew a family boundary ourselves, which
plate the one significant cell came from, and the fact that the electronic
coordinates rest on a cached calculation whose program version we did not
record.

\textbf{On data integrity, and on this manuscript's own history.} This analysis
was audited four times before submission and each audit changed the numbers, so
we state what happened rather than present the final version as though it had
come out that way.

The first audit found that the numeric parser had split thousands separators in
the source table, so that ``12,000~ppm'' was read as 12. The second, of chemical
identity rather than of numbers, found two records whose stored structure
belonged to a different compound; both are excluded. The third found four further
faults in how the regulatory table was read, all of one kind: treating a printed
table as though its cells always held plain numbers. Footnote lines beginning
\textit{AEGL 3 - 10 min = ...} were parsed as though they were table rows, which
turned duration labels into values and gave propane and butane ten-minute
AEGL-3 values of 10~ppm against the true 33,000 and 77,000~ppm. Nine compounds
whose ten-minute cell reads \textit{see below}, the agency's marker for a value
above half the lower explosive limit, had no value collected at all, among them
methanol, acetone, benzene and n-hexane. Rows whose longer durations read
\textit{NR} were discarded whole, which silently dropped three arsenical
vesicants and BZ from the held-out agent set. And one row broken across two lines
by the document's text layer was lost.

The fourth audit was of our own statistics rather than of the data, and it is the
reason the paper now says what it says. It found the functional dependence in the
electronic descriptor set, the pairwise-distance pseudoreplication in the array
analysis, our own misuse of an algebraic bound on the centred cosines, the
absence of any multiplicity
correction across eighteen tests we had in fact computed, and the fact that we had
never run the panel that includes the other compounds the literature treats as
simulants. An earlier version
of this manuscript reported that cell as a result and built a thesis on it. None
of those five problems is visible in that version, and together they remove the
thesis. We have rewritten the paper around what the data support, and we report
the earlier reading alongside it rather than deleting it, because how a nominal
result of this kind arises is part of what a reader of this literature needs.

The extraction is now cell-based and refuses any row that does not resolve into
five duration cells, and it is validated over the whole table by a second reader
that shares neither the text-extraction engine nor the algorithm, reading values
by character column position rather than by token order. The two readers agree on
all 250 entries for which both read a value from a table cell, on all 257 that
resolved to a structure and on all nineteen panel values.

\textbf{Dual-use.} The optimisation direction is fixed throughout and stated in
the manuscript: detection-relevant fidelity is maximised while phosphorus
electrophilicity, acetylcholinesterase reactivity, alkylating capacity and
predicted mammalian toxicity are minimised. The structural filter excludes
candidates and never orders them. The one ordering the paper publishes ranks
compounds that have already cleared that filter by resemblance to the agents,
and every compound in it is an industrial chemical in commerce carrying a
published regulatory exposure value. We did not extend the toxicity model to the
organophosphorus class, because a predictor of mammalian lethality evaluated over
structures not yet made, inside a loop that proposes them, is the configuration
this work is built to avoid. We cite the existing literature on organophosphorus
lethality modelling, including our own, rather than implying that none exists.

The acute toxicity axis, the random-forest interpolation of it, the decoupling
test between fidelity and toxicity, and the phosphorus-local reactivity analysis
are in the Supplementary Material. They stand on their own data and are
summarised where the main text needs them; we placed them there because the
argument of the paper turns on the composition of the comparison panel and does
not depend on them.

The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and has been
approved by all six authors. The authors declare no competing financial interest,
and the work received no specific funding. Analysis code, descriptor tables,
parsed toxicity values with their sources and all figure scripts are deposited in
the public repository cited in the manuscript and also accompany the submission.
The designation and Mantel permutation tests are exhaustive. Seven further
analyses are sampled rather than enumerated, at between 1000 and 20000 draws
each, and the Supporting Information names every one with its draw count; the
remainder are deterministic given the stated seeds.

Thank you for considering this work.
