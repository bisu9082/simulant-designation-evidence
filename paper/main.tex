\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Chemical warfare agent detection is developed almost entirely on simulants:
compounds accepted as agent-like and safe to handle. We tested that
acceptance. In the public standard-setting record, no incumbent carries a
designation that names detection: one is designated for filtration, one's
patent text we could not obtain, and the third has no designating document we
could find; the Army procedure governing simulant selection is not public, so
this bounds the claim.
For eleven authentic agents we then computed structural and GFN2-xTB electronic
descriptors and asked, by exact permutation over every designation, whether
those three sit closer to the agents than the panel's other non-agents. On the
primary thirty-five-compound panel, which adds five compounds the sensor
literature also calls simulants, no agent has a valid match and no test reaches
significance. Resampling the descriptors gives the incumbent
three a median of zero matched agents against five for a set of eight containing
them, and neither count is unusual for its size: the zero sits at $p=0.48$
against an expected 1.0, the five at $p=0.07$ against 1.92 once the null
contains the incumbents. What the extended panel changes is the comparison
rather than the chemistry, since diisopropyl fluorophosphate is nearer to three
G agents than any incumbent is. A
twenty-eight-compound panel omitting those seven yields three matches and a
nominal $p$ of 0.025 that no correction over eighteen tests leaves significant,
while relabelling the two pesticides as simulants produces an adjusted 0.0008. A defensible claim must name the
measurement it rests on and fix panel, space, statistic and weighting in
advance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Work on detecting chemical warfare agents (CWAs) is rarely done with CWAs.
Outside a small number of licensed facilities, sensor arrays, sorbents and
sampling protocols are developed, optimised and published using simulants:
compounds chosen to behave like an agent while being safe enough for an ordinary
laboratory. The choice of simulant therefore sets a ceiling on what a published
detection result can be taken to mean, and that choice is made by a convention
rather than by a measurement.

The dependence is structural, not incidental. The standing reviews of agent
destruction and detection organise a literature in which reactivity and sensing
chemistry are established on surrogates and extrapolated to agents
\cite{kim2011,jang2015}, and the same pattern runs through the chromogenic,
fluorogenic and supramolecular recognition chemistry developed for nerve agents
\cite{royo2007,sambrook2013,eubanks2007}. Much field instrumentation descends
from a colorimetric sensor-array lineage established on ordinary analytes
\cite{rakow2000,askim2013} and transferred to the agent problem, so the
compounds an array is characterised on are inherited rather than fixed by the
chemistry. Our own work sits on both sides
\cite{hazref,multiplexref,ondeviceref,group3ref}. What a simulant is for differs
by subfield: for environmental fate the criterion has been reviewed explicitly
\cite{bartelthunt2008}, in metal--organic framework adsorption the simulant
establishes the mechanism \cite{mondloch2015}, and in decontamination DMMP is the
compound whose removal is quantified \cite{ebeam}. In every case the inference
runs from the simulant to the agent, and the warrant for it is a resemblance
argument.

The convention is property matching. A recent critical review of liquid,
low-toxicity simulants states the criterion plainly: align physicochemical
properties such as hydrophobicity, volatility and solubility with those of the
agent, with the simulants it assesses described as presenting minimal toxicity
risks \cite{webb2025}. That review is confined to liquid-phase sampling, but the
same criterion underwrites vapour-phase detection, where the quantity of
interest is not partitioning but a chemical response.

Two assumptions are built into this practice, and both are testable. The first
is that resemblance in structural and physicochemical descriptors implies
resemblance in the property a sensor reports; for adsorption in metal--organic
frameworks this has been examined directly, four common simulants and two nerve
agents simulated across 2969 frameworks reproducing agent adsorption behaviour
only partially \cite{agrawal2018}, while the analogous question for the
electronic properties governing a chemical response has not been asked. A
cheminformatic attempt at the selection problem predates this one, in this
journal: Lavoie et al.\ applied the chemical similarity search used in
pharmaceutical screening to simulant identification, with the stated aim of
finding compounds close to the agents but less toxic \cite{lavoie2011}. That aim
is the one the field still has, and what the present work adds is that the
compounds the detection literature settled on were not reached by that procedure
or by any comparable one we could find. The second assumption is that a simulant
is safe, usually supported by the absence of an agent-class hazard label rather
than by a toxicity value. That is the practice in detection; the
human-decontamination literature instead screens candidates against toxicity
explicitly and reports the attrition, one review discounting 78 of 171 chemicals
on the risk to volunteers \cite{james2021}.

Both assumptions can be checked against measurements that already exist: the
electronic structure of small molecules is accessible at semi-empirical
tight-binding cost \cite{gfnxtb2017,bannwarth2019}, and acute inhalation
toxicity has been derived by a formal regulatory process for several hundred
chemicals including the agents themselves \cite{watson2006}. We ask where
simulants and agents sit relative to one another, how much of that answer is
fixed by the data rather than by the analyst's choice of panel, space and
statistic, and whether the incumbents are safe on a value or on an absence. The
first question turned out to have a different shape than we expected. We began by
comparing descriptor spaces, found a difference, and then found on audit that it
did not survive extending the compound panel, correcting for the number of
statistics computed, or removing a descriptor that was an exact function of two
others in the same space. What the data support is reported below as such, with
the earlier reading alongside it, because the way a nominal result of this kind
arises is part of what a reader of this literature needs.

\paragraph{Dual-use statement.} Throughout, the optimisation direction is fixed:
fidelity to the detection-relevant properties is to be maximised while
phosphorus electrophilicity, acetylcholinesterase reactivity, alkylating
capacity and predicted mammalian toxicity are to be minimised. No analysis here
ranks compounds by predicted mammalian toxicity. One analysis does order
compounds on a reactivity surrogate: Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-res:plocal}
reports the condensed Fukui index at phosphorus for the six phosphorus-bearing
compounds of the panel, which is defined with respect to attack on phosphorus
and is therefore an acetylcholinesterase-relevant quantity. It covers only
compounds whose structures and agent status are already public, it is used to
show that the incumbent simulant sits closer to the agents on that axis than the
fidelity argument assumes, and it is not extended to any novel structure. The
structural filter in Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-sec:alerts} excludes candidates and never
orders them. One ordering is published, in
Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-res:h1}, and it ranks compounds that have cleared
that filter by resemblance to the agents in the electronic space. Every
compound in it is an industrial chemical in commerce that already carries a
published regulatory exposure value, which is how it entered the pool at all,
and the selection that produced the list is a safety threshold rather than a
reactivity one. The alert patterns themselves are the agent-class motifs of the
published structural-alert literature cited in Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-sec:alerts}, and
Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-sec:alerts} states in prose what each tier covers, so releasing the
patterns with the code adds no structure-activity information that the text does
not already give. We did not extend the toxicity
model of Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-sec:qsar} to the organophosphorus class,
because a model predicting mammalian lethality for structures not yet made would
invert the direction of the work. That a predictive model trained for safety can
be run in reverse is not hypothetical \cite{urbina2022}, and how much of such a
method to disclose is itself a live problem \cite{cbrndisclose}. Mechanistic
modelling of organophosphorus lethality is established and separately published,
including by the present authors \cite{qsarld50}; what is withheld is its use as
a design objective over novel structures, not the literature's existence.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Compound set and the two panels}
\label{meth:panels}
Eleven authentic agents are common to both panels: sarin GB, soman GD, tabun GA,
cyclosarin GF, VX, sulfur mustard HD, lewisite~1 L, hydrogen cyanide AC,
cyanogen chloride CK, phosgene CG and chloropicrin PS. Three compounds are
treated as the incumbent simulants: DMMP, dichloromethane and dipropylene glycol
monomethyl ether (DPGME). We call these three incumbent rather than designated,
and the word is operational: they are the compounds the sensing literature we
surveyed runs its arrays and sorbents against, which is a statement about usage
and not about standing. That usage is thin for two of them, resting on one
metal-oxide sensor study for dichloromethane \cite{lee2011sno2} and on a
training patent for DPGME \cite{seitzinger2006simulant}, and a different survey
could reasonably return a different three. Section~\ref{res:docs} reports what
searching for a designating instrument returned, since that search is a result
of this work rather than a premise of it.

The two panels differ only in the non-agent pool, and the difference is the
point. The twenty-eight-compound panel adds fourteen interferents and solvents
routinely reported in sensor-array selectivity panels. The
thirty-five-compound panel adds, to those, five further compounds used as
simulants in the sensor, sorbent and decontamination literature (diisopropyl
fluorophosphate DFP, diethyl chlorophosphate DCP, diethyl cyanophosphonate
DCNP\cite{lee2021tabun,jang2014tabun}, 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide CEES and
methyl salicylate), classified as simulants rather than as interferents, and
two organophosphorus pesticides, dichlorvos and malathion. We class the
pesticides as interferents because the literature we surveyed does not use them
to stand in for an agent, and flag the decision as contestable on two counts:
malathion appears in the NIOSH document's barrier-breakthrough tables, and both
carry the phosphoryl motif the structural filter of Supporting Information
Section~\ref{S-meth:filter} treats as agent-class.
Section~\ref{res:panels} reports how sensitive the result is to exactly this
kind of decision. The thirty-five-compound panel is the primary one, because a
comparison asking whether three compounds are close to the agents while
excluding the obvious alternatives is not a test of those three. We report the
twenty-eight-compound panel throughout with equal numerical detail, because what
it shows beside the larger one is the paper's result and not support for a
conclusion. All class labels were read from a single canonical table and never
assigned by hand.

Panel, space, statistic and descriptor weighting were not fixed before the
analysis was run, and this paper's conclusion is that they should be. We state
them here so that the standard we recommend can be applied to the work
recommending it. The merged space is reported alongside both component spaces
rather than selected among them. Six distance statistics were computed and none
was nominated in advance, which Section~\ref{res:panels} reports the consequence
of and Supporting Information Table~\ref{S-tab:family} prices. Equal weight per
descriptor was the default we inherited rather than one we chose, and Supporting
Information Section~\ref{S-res:blockbalance} reports the alternative.

\subsection{Descriptors and descriptor spaces}
Structural and physicochemical descriptors (51 after excluding run metadata)
were computed with RDKit \cite{rdkit} from canonical SMILES; electronic
descriptors were computed with GFN2-xTB \cite{bannwarth2019} in the gas phase,
since vapour-phase response is the property of interest. Three spaces are
compared: structural, electronic and merged. Interpretable descriptors were used
throughout in preference to a substructural fingerprint, because the quantity of
interest is position along named physical axes rather than a similarity score.
Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-meth:descriptors} gives the descriptor
list, the conformer treatment, the electronic protocol and the correlation and
exact-dependence filters that leave 38 structural coordinates on the primary
panel and 35 on the twenty-eight-compound one. Distances are Euclidean on
standardised descriptors, normalised by the median nearest-neighbour separation
of the panel in hand, so a normalised distance is comparable within a panel and
not between panels.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbour comparison and permutation test}
\label{sec:perm}
For each authentic agent we recorded the distance to the nearest incumbent
simulant and to the nearest interferent. With fourteen interferents against
three simulants the minimum of fourteen draws is smaller than the minimum of
three by construction, so a paired test of the two distances is confounded by
group size. We therefore enumerated all ways of designating three of the
non-agents as simulants, computed the same statistic for each, and report the
rank of the actual designation as an exact $p$-value: $\binom{24}{3}=2024$
designations on the primary panel and $\binom{17}{3}=680$ on the
twenty-eight-compound one. The null expectation for ``an interferent is closer''
follows from the group sizes alone and is 9.63 of 11 on the primary panel and
9.06 on the smaller one. The hypothesis under test is directional, since
property matching is claimed to place simulants closer to the agents than
arbitrary compounds would be, so the reported $p$ is one-sided. The choice is
consequential and we give all three conventions: scored two-sided as distance
from the null mean the same statistic gives 0.044, 0.482 and 0.028 for the
structural, electronic and merged spaces, and at twice the smaller tail 0.056,
0.550 and 0.050, so whether the structural value sits inside a conventional
threshold depends on a choice a reader may make differently. This is the first
of the analyst choices catalogued in Section~\ref{res:panels} and the only one
with a defensible default.

\subsection{Match criterion}
\label{meth:match}
Nearest-neighbour rank alone is not sufficient: in the 38-dimensional merged
space of the twenty-eight-compound panel a ``nearest'' simulant at a distance of
11.8 is not a match. We therefore normalised each distance by the median
nearest-neighbour distance of the same space and define a valid match as a
normalised distance $\le 2$ together with a distance smaller than that to every
compound outside the designated set. The two conditions are not equally fragile:
the threshold is a convention, the second condition is not.

The comparison set named in the second condition can be read three ways and the
reading changes the count, so we declare ours. We take it to be every compound
that is neither an authentic agent nor a designated simulant, twenty-one
compounds on the primary panel and fourteen on the twenty-eight-compound one. A
narrower reading restricts it to the sixteen compounds we labelled interferents,
which on the primary panel drops the five literature compounds from the
comparison. A literal reading of ``outside the designated set'' would admit the
other ten agents as well, thirty-two compounds, which asks whether a simulant is
nearer to its agent than other agents are and is a different question. The three
readings give zero, three and zero valid matches on the primary panel; three,
three and zero on the twenty-eight-compound panel; and five, five and two once
the five literature compounds are themselves designated
(\texttt{outputs/match\_rule\_audit.json}). The narrower reading raises the
primary-panel count from zero to three only because it removes those five from
the comparison, which is the panel-composition effect of
Section~\ref{res:panels} acting on the criterion rather than on the chemistry.
We use the first reading throughout and report the other two so that the choice
is visible. The criterion is a statement about proximity in this descriptor
space and nothing further: it does not certify that a simulant reproduces an
agent's response on a given instrument. On the primary panel the incumbent
simulants fail the second condition for all eleven agents, so the count there is
zero at every threshold, including an unbounded one.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
All analyses are deterministic given the seed. The designation and Mantel
permutation tests are exhaustive; the eight-simulant designation null and the
random-subspace controls are sampled, at 20000 and 2000 draws. Numerical work
used NumPy 2.5.3 \cite{numpy2020}, SciPy 1.18.1 \cite{scipy2020}, pandas 3.0.5
\cite{mckinney2010} and scikit-learn 1.9.1, figures Matplotlib 3.11.2
\cite{matplotlib2007}, and descriptors RDKit 2026.03.6 \cite{rdkit}. Code,
descriptor tables, parsed toxicity values with their sources and the figure
scripts are in the repository cited under Data and code availability.

\section{Results}

\subsection{What the designating documents say, and for which measurement}
\label{res:docs}

Searching for an instrument that designates the three incumbent compounds as
chemical warfare agent simulants, we found that two carry a documented
designation for a purpose other than the one they now serve, and the third
carries none. The record separates into three grades, set out in
Table~\ref{tab:docs}: a compound named for a stated measurement, a compound
recorded only as having been used, and a compound with no document at all.

DMMP was identified as a test simulant for canister and cartridge filtration
against GB and HD in a NIOSH respirator programme \cite{niosh2002simulant}, and
appears again in the same document's candidate list for the GB and HD permeation
study. That document also states that the simulant information it provides is
not to be used as a recommendation of any simulant, and the property at issue in
both roles is breakthrough through a barrier rather than detection chemistry.
DPGME appears in the title of a patent assigned to the United States Department
of the Army for a chemical agent simulant training
composition.\cite{seitzinger2006simulant} We could not obtain the patent text:
Google Patents, Justia and the USPTO full-text service all refused, and only the
bibliographic record is confirmed, so neither the agent class nor the
measurement the compound was selected for is established from the document
itself and we make no claim about either. For dichloromethane we found no
designating document of any kind: it is absent from the NIOSH document and from
the Man-in-Simulant Test programme, and present in this role, as far as we can
establish, only in the research literature, where it is run as a simulant
alongside DMMP and DPGME on metal-oxide gas sensors \cite{lee2011sno2}. That
study puts a fourth compound in the same role, acetonitrile, which the panel
used here classes as an interferent, so the literature usage does not resolve to
a fixed set either.

A negative claim about documents is only as good as its scope, and ours has a
hole in it which is the most important document of all. We searched the
standard-setting and test-procedure literature reachable without a subscription
or a clearance, listed in Supporting Information
Section~\ref{S-res:searchscope}. TOP 08-2-196, \emph{Simulant Selection for
Laboratory, Chamber, and Field Testing} \cite{top082196}, is the Army's
procedure for exactly the question this section asks, and we could not obtain
it; we know its title and its role only because TOP 08-2-188 CN1, which governs
chemical point detector vapour testing, defers simulant selection to it
\cite{top082188}. That public procedure also states a criterion about detection
rather than about barriers, that the simulant ``should produce a signature
signal similar to the one from the threat analyte''. What follows is a limit on
the claim rather than a retraction of it. We can say that the three compounds
the sensor literature runs on carry no public designation naming a detection
measurement, and that the designations we did find name other measurements; we
cannot say that no such instrument exists, and TOP 08-2-196 is where one would
be. A reader with access to it should treat this section as a question put to
that document rather than as an answer about it, and the measurements in the
rest of the paper do not depend on how that question comes out.

Two observations follow. A designation is specific to a measurement: a compound
chosen because it breaks through a carbon bed like sarin has been validated for
adsorption and not for an optical or electrochemical response, so the sensor
literature using these three is using designations issued for other purposes.
And the compounds with the clearest designations are not these three, while the
clearest of them is not clear in the way we first read it. Methyl salicylate is
the simulant of the Army's Man-in-Simulant Test programme, carried into a
consensus standard that fixes the measurement as vapour penetration of a
protective ensemble \cite{astmf2588}; yet the National Academies assessment of
that programme states that its selection ``was based on its historical use as an
agent simulant'' rather than on a validation against the agents, and records
that the assumptions behind its use ``have not been tested''
\cite{nrc1997mist}. The compound with the strongest instrument behind it
carries, in the same literature, an explicit statement that the instrument rests
on prior use. That is the pattern of this section rather than an exception to
it, and it is why Table~\ref{tab:docs} grades what a document does and not how
authoritative it looks. A recent survey states the first half of this in general
form, that simulants have generally been selected on the basis of their
historical use \cite{lawrence2025simulants}; it records the absence of a
development standard for \emph{explosive} simulants, not for chemical agent
simulants, so the absence we report here rests on our own search and not on a
prior claim. Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-res:paneldetail} lists the
compounds the NIOSH document and the Man-in-Simulant Test name.

\subsection{What the comparison contains decides the answer}
\label{res:panels}

On the thirty-five-compound panel, no authentic agent has a valid match to an
incumbent simulant. The mechanism is one of panel composition rather than
chemistry, and it is not the one our earlier version reported: the compounds did
not become less faithful, but diisopropyl fluorophosphate is present and is
nearer to sarin, soman and cyclosarin than DMMP is, and the match criterion
requires a simulant to be nearer than every compound not designated one
(Figure~\ref{fig:main}a). Admitting the five as simulants gives five matched
agents of eleven, against three on the twenty-eight-compound panel and zero on
the primary one. The zero does not depend on the threshold: no incumbent
simulant is the nearest non-agent compound to any of the eleven agents, so the
count stays at zero however far the threshold is relaxed. Resampling the
merged-space descriptors gives the incumbent three a median of zero matched
agents and the eight-compound set containing them a median of five, and neither
count departs from a null built to resemble it ($p=0.479$ and $p=0.068$). Because
zero is the smallest attainable count, $p=0.479$ is the smallest lower-tail
value this comparison can ever return: it reports a comparison that cannot reach
significance at any conventional level, whatever the data. Supporting Information
Section~\ref{S-res:paneldetail} gives the intervals, the per-agent reliabilities,
which reverse what the point estimates suggest, and the controls on dimension
and subspace.

Every exact permutation test we can run, across four panel and designation
configurations, three spaces and six statistics, is in Supporting Information
Table~\ref{S-tab:ablation}, in full rather than selected from, because the
pattern across the cells is the finding. On the primary panel no test reaches
nominal significance, so in no space and under no statistic are the incumbent
simulants distinguishable from compounds drawn at random. Classing the five
literature compounds as simulants recovers two nominal values below 0.05, so the
difficulty is not that the test cannot detect anything on this panel. On the
twenty-eight-compound panel, which differs only in omitting those five and the
two pesticides, the incumbents beat random compounds under one of six statistics
in the structural space and three of six in the merged space.

Correcting for the number of times we looked removes almost all of it, and the
exception is instructive. On the primary panel the question does not arise, since
the smallest nominal value is already 0.192. The five accounting schemes of
Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-meth:multiplicity} matter only for the
twenty-eight-compound panel, where the eighteen-test family gives a smallest
adjusted value of 0.163, correcting the six statistics within each space gives
0.077, and the Westfall-Young min-$P$ procedure, which accounts for the
dependence between the tests exactly rather than approximating it, gives 0.079.
That is the closest any correction brings the panel to the conventional
threshold without crossing it. The fifth scheme behaves differently: had the mean
difference been specified in advance as the single statistic, three tests would
have been run and the twenty-eight-compound structural and merged results would
survive at $q=0.042$, while neither thirty-five-compound configuration would. We
did not specify a statistic in advance; we computed six and chose among them
after seeing them, which is the circumstance the correction is for. One analysis,
one data set, five defensible ways of accounting for the search, and a result
significant under one and not the other four. The declaration that would have
made the difference is one nobody in this literature makes, and it has to be made
before the data are seen.

One label decision carries more than any of this. Section~\ref{meth:panels}
classes dichlorvos and malathion as interferents and records that decision as
contestable. Classing them as simulants instead, which moves two labels and
changes nothing else, gives a smallest nominal value of 0.00006 and a
Benjamini-Hochberg value of 0.0008 over the same eighteen tests, and it is the
only configuration in this work that survives every one of the five accounting
schemes. The direction is not surprising, since both pesticides carry the
phosphoryl motif the agents carry, and that is the point. Nor is the relabelling
arbitrary, and here we correct ourselves: we described the two as compounds
nobody uses as simulants, which is wrong for one of them, since the NIOSH
document lists malathion in its GB simulant column and reports breakthrough
times for it through protective clothing materials \cite{niosh2002simulant}. That
strengthens rather than weakens the point. A defensible label change, made by an
analyst with a citation in hand, moves this analysis from nothing at $q=0.630$ to
$q=0.0008$, and the practice records neither that a label was moved nor which
one. A designated set can be made to look supported by choosing what goes into
it.

What the one surviving nominal result carries is narrower than it sounds, and
the sign of the statistic says so. The observed mean difference in the structural
space is $+0.195$: the designated simulants are on average \emph{further} from
the agents than the nearest compound outside the designated set. What the test
detects is that they are less far than a triple drawn at random from a pool
assembled out of interferents and solvents, which were chosen for this panel
because they are not like the agents. Against that reference class a single
organophosphorus compound is close to sufficient.

\subsection{How many agents have a valid simulant depends on the panel}
\label{res:match}

Supporting Information Table~\ref{S-tab:match} lists, for each agent on the
twenty-eight-compound panel, the nearest incumbent simulant in the merged space
as a multiple of that space's typical nearest-neighbour separation. Sarin
(1.187), soman (1.446) and cyclosarin (1.594) are matched, all three by DMMP;
tabun at 2.004 and VX at 3.057 are not. Tabun misses the threshold of 2 by
0.004, and we flag that rather than report the count as firm: an earlier version
of this analysis, which retained a structural coordinate that is an exact
function of two others, put tabun at 1.9948 and counted four matches here instead
of three. A count that moves when a redundant coordinate is removed should be
read as marginal, which is the same lesson the rest of this paper draws about the
panel. DPGME is nominally VX's nearest simulant in all three spaces, but 3.057
typical separations is not a match in any useful sense, and its designating
document is a patent whose text we could not
obtain,\cite{seitzinger2006simulant} so there is no designated class for these
spaces to resolve or fail to resolve.

The count is stable but not invariant, and the two spaces admit tabun at
different bars: in the merged space the matched set runs from \{GB, GD\} at a
threshold of 1.5 to \{GB, GD, GA, GF\} at 2.5 and 3.0, tabun entering only once
the bar passes its value of 2.004, and over the thresholds tested the count does
not exceed four of eleven in any space
(\texttt{outputs/threshold\_sweep.json}). The electronic space is the exception
and the informative one: there the count is one at every threshold tested,
hydrogen cyanide matched by dichloromethane at 1.407, because acetonitrile sits
closer to sarin, soman and cyclosarin than DMMP does
and all three fail the threshold-free condition outright. Crossing the
threshold-independent choices instead, three readings of the comparison set by
two correlation filters by three panels plus the seven sparse-indicator
encodings, gives twenty-five cells in which the matched set takes seven distinct
values; five agents have no valid simulant in any of them (Supporting
Information Section~\ref{S-meth:failaudit}).

The dependence on the panel is sharper than the dependence on the threshold. On
the thirty-five-compound panel, with the incumbent three still the only
simulants and the five literature compounds in the comparison pool, the merged
space returns no valid match for any of the eleven agents: DMMP loses GB, GD and
GF to diisopropyl fluorophosphate, which is nearer to all three. Admitting the
five as simulants gives five matches of eleven, more than the incumbent set
achieves on either panel. VX remains unmatched in every panel and space we
examined. We draw no conclusion from this that the field should switch
compounds, because the five were not selected by any procedure we can inspect
either, and Section~\ref{res:panels} applies to them exactly as it applies to the
incumbent three. What the comparison establishes is narrower: the statement
``three of eleven agents have a valid simulant'' is true of one panel, false of a
second, and replaced by a different list on a third, which is the same
instability the permutation tests report and not an independent finding.

\subsection{What the array actually measures}
\label{res:measured}

Every argument above rests on descriptor distances, and the premise that one of
those spaces corresponds to what a vapour-phase sensor reports is tested in
Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-res:arraydetail} against measured
colorimetric response for twelve panel compounds over 29 dyes. It does not
resolve the question. Of twelve panel-channel-space cells three reach a nominal
$p<0.05$ and they do not agree on a space; correcting
over the twelve leaves a smallest adjusted
value of 0.117, and all three lie in the plate set containing
no simulant, so they compare one agent with another. The six cells that bear on
the premise are the six in the set holding DMMP, and all six are null. The exact
Mantel over all 720 relabellings of six compounds detects $|\rho| \ge 0.85$ at
the Benjamini--Hochberg level these cells are judged against, so these data
exclude a strong association and nothing weaker. The premise is therefore
untested rather than supported, and every distance-based claim in this paper
inherits that. Three artifacts are reported there because our own first version
contained all three.

\subsection{The toxicity half of the criterion}
\label{res:toxsummary}



The criterion has a second half: that compounds chosen on physicochemical
grounds present minimal toxicity risk \cite{webb2025}. We tested it on the same
panel and report it in full in the Supporting Information
(Sections~\ref{S-meth:aegl}, \ref{S-res:safety}, \ref{S-sec:qsar} and
\ref{S-res:h1}). Three results are needed here. The two incumbents differ in
how well documented they are and not only in how hazardous: dichloromethane has
a regulator-derived acute inhalation value at the 96th percentile of a
207-compound pool, and DMMP has neither an acute value nor a chronic one. A
model cannot fill that gap: a random forest trained on 230 AEGL chemicals
reaches $R^{2}=0.458$ and then predicts every one of twenty held-out military
agents safer than measured, by factors of 2.5 to 2600, while certifying fourteen
of them as inside its applicability domain, a tail regression that an
input-space domain check cannot see. And fidelity and acute toxicity show no
detectable monotonic association across the 207 compounds carrying both measured
axes (Spearman $r=+0.062$, 95\% CI $-0.077$ to $+0.199$), an interval that
bounds the association rather than establishing independence. We draw no
candidate recommendation from any of it.

\section{Discussion}

The criterion by which low-toxicity simulants are chosen is stated clearly
enough to test \cite{webb2025}: align hydrophobicity, volatility and solubility
with the agent. What the practice does not state is what the chosen compounds
should be compared against, and that decides the answer. On a panel containing
the compounds the field itself uses as simulants, the three incumbents match no
agent and no test reaches nominal significance; on a panel omitting them, three
agents are matched and the smallest nominal value is 0.025. The mechanism is not
a story about fidelity: on a single standardisation basis the incumbents do not
move between the panels at all. What the larger panel supplies is diisopropyl
fluorophosphate, so a criterion that asks a simulant to beat the alternatives
returns nothing. A designation defended by a comparison that excludes the
alternatives is not defended.

An earlier version of this work reported the twenty-eight-compound structural
cell as the finding and built a thesis on the contrast between it and the
electronic space. Three things undo that reading, and each is a mistake another
group could make the same way. The value does not survive the eighteen tests of
its own family ($q = 0.163$), although it would have survived at $q = 0.042$ had
the statistic been fixed in advance, which demonstrates the paper's argument
rather than defending the value. It sits at the 22.4th percentile of 2000
arbitrary three-descriptor slices of the same block. And it is specific to the
panel that omits the five. What does not undo the reading, and what we reported
as though it did, is the share of those slices reaching $p<0.05$: corrected
against the 680 designations that share generates, the incumbent designation
ranks seventh. Rebuilding the electronic space at the density-functional level
moves it from $p=0.275$ to 0.0368 while the merged space holds at 0.0250
(Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-meth:dft}), so we withdraw the contrast
between the spaces as something to build on.

What remains is a claim about comparison rather than about chemistry. On the
primary panel we cannot establish that the incumbent simulants are closer to the
agents than compounds drawn at random, and we cannot establish the opposite
either. What we can establish is that the compounds which would make the
comparison informative were not in it.

The documentary record explains how that arose, and it is not a story about
carelessness. One of the three was designated carefully for a measurement that
is not the one it is now asked to support (Section~\ref{res:docs}): DMMP, for
adsorptive breakthrough on a filter bed \cite{niosh2002simulant}. For the other
two the public record establishes no measurement at all. The compounds then
carried their status into sensor development, where the governing property is the
response of a dye or an electrode rather than a breakthrough time, and nothing
in the original validation speaks to that. The absence is specific rather than
general: a safety-screened repository of candidate simulants exists
\cite{james2021} and a descriptor-based selection procedure appeared in this
journal fifteen years ago \cite{lavoie2011}; what is missing is any route by
which either reached the compounds the detection literature settled on.

That mechanism is general. A simulant is validated against a measurement rather
than against an agent, and treating the validation as a property of the compound,
so that it travels into a new application, produces a working set whose standing
no descriptor space can reproduce. The corrective is not a replacement compound
but a declaration: which measurement a simulant was validated against, and a
panel, space and statistic fixed before a claim that it is valid for a different
one is tested. Agrawal et al.~\cite{agrawal2018} reach the same place from the
other direction, adequacy being a property of the compound together with the
measurement it stands in for. Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-disc:detail} sets
out what follows for practice, including why the global electrophilicity index
should not enter a descriptor space and why the safety of a simulant is a
question about evidence before it is one about hazard.

We did not attempt to improve the toxicity model by training on organophosphorus
compounds, and the distinction is narrower than a refusal to model the chemistry.
Mechanistic structure--activity models of organophosphorus lethality exist and we
have contributed to them \cite{qsarld50}; such a model assesses compounds that
already exist. What we decline to build is the same instrument turned into a
design objective, a predictor of mammalian lethality evaluated over structures
not yet made inside a loop that proposes them \cite{urbina2022}. Reporting that
gap rather than closing it is the correct outcome \cite{cbrndisclose}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The documentary findings of Section~\ref{res:docs} rest
on public sources, and the scope of that search is stated there: the claim is
that no public instrument designates these three compounds for the use the
sensor literature makes of them, not that none does. The test is sensitive to
effects smaller than the one at issue, reaching $p<0.05$ once the incumbents are
moved 0.23 typical separations closer against an actual median deficit of 0.493
(Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-res:mde}). The agent set is eleven
compounds and the nearest-neighbour statistics inherit that: leaving out one
agent at a time moves the structural $p$ between 0.020 and 0.186. Measured
toxicity values exist for a subset of the panel only, DMMP among the compounds
without one, so the two axes do not run over the same compounds. The four
configurations reported sample the configuration space rather than census it, so
the panels should be read as demonstrations of panel dependence rather than as a
census of compounds used as simulants. One omission is systematic: probe
chemistries for lewisite are developed against arsenic trichloride
\cite{lee2016lewisite}, which is in neither panel. GFN2-xTB is semi-empirical
and the quantities used are relative within a consistent protocol rather than
absolute; Supporting Information Section~\ref{S-disc:detail} gives the remaining
limitations in full.

\section{Conclusions}

Simulant validity cannot be treated as an intrinsic property of a compound. It
depends on the measurement it is intended to reproduce and on the comparison
against which that claim is evaluated, and the practice of choosing chemical
warfare agent simulants records neither. On a panel of thirty-five compounds
that includes the five the sensor, sorbent and decontamination literature also
uses as simulants, not one of eleven authentic agents has a valid match to an
incumbent simulant and not one of eighteen tests reaches nominal significance.
The incumbents are no further from the agents on that panel than on the smaller
one; diisopropyl fluorophosphate is simply nearer. Omitting those five restores
three matches and a nominal structural $p$ of 0.028, a value at the 22.4th
percentile of 2000 arbitrary three-descriptor subspaces of the same block which
no correction over the eighteen tests run leaves significant.

The documentary record gives the reason. DMMP was designated for filtration
breakthrough testing, which is not detection chemistry; the text of the DPGME
patent we could not obtain, so no measurement is established for it; for
dichloromethane we found no designating document at all. A simulant is validated
against a measurement, and that validation does not travel with the compound
into a new application.

The absence of evidence is not evidence of unsuitability, and we draw no
conclusion that the incumbents are poor choices. What we conclude is narrower
and addressed to method. A simulant claim is testable, this field has the data to
test it, and the test returns whatever the analyst's undeclared choices imply:
moving two labels, from interferent to simulant, is enough to turn a result that
survives no correction into one with an adjusted value of 0.0008. The practical
form of that conclusion is a reporting rule. A simulant result should name the
measurement the compound was validated against and the comparison set it was
tested in, and should fix the panel, the descriptor space, the statistic and the
descriptor weighting before the test is run, and should state how any
conformer-dependent descriptor was taken. We add the last because we had not
counted it ourselves until a reproduction check found it (Supporting Information
Section~\ref{S-meth:conformer}); unlike the other four it does not move the
answer here, but nothing in the design guaranteed that. Until those are declared
in advance, a statement that a compound is a validated simulant reports a
convention rather than a measurement.

Findings from the toxicity axis are in the Supporting Information and do not
depend on the contested comparison. The two incumbents differ in how well
documented they are and not only in how hazardous, dichloromethane carrying a
regulator-derived acute inhalation value and DMMP carrying none of any kind; a
random forest trained on 230 AEGL chemicals predicts all twenty held-out
military agents safer than measured, by factors of 2.5 to 2600, so predicted
toxicity cannot stand in for the missing values; and fidelity and acute toxicity
show no detectable monotonic association across the 207 compounds carrying both
axes, an interval that bounds the association rather than establishing
independence. We propose no replacement compound, because proposing one would
repeat the error this paper is about.
