\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Chemical warfare agent detection is developed almost entirely on simulants:
compounds accepted as agent-like and safe enough to handle in an ordinary
laboratory. We tested whether that acceptance has a measurable basis. For eleven
authentic agents we computed structural and GFN2-xTB electronic descriptors and
asked, by exact permutation over every possible designation, whether three
incumbent simulants sit closer to the agents than compounds drawn at random. On
our primary thirty-five-compound panel, which includes five further compounds
the sensor literature also uses as simulants, no agent has a valid match at any
threshold and no test falls below $p=0.15$. Resampling the descriptors gives the
incumbent three a median of zero matched agents against five for the eight the
field uses, the difference positive in 1000 of 1000 replicates. The distances
barely move between panels; what changes is that something closer is present. A
twenty-eight-compound panel omitting those five yields three matches and a
smallest nominal $p$ of 0.025, surviving one of five defensible multiplicity
schemes. Two of the three were designated for measurements that are not
detection, and the third was not designated at all. A defensible claim must name
the originating measurement and fix the panel, space and statistic in advance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Work on detecting chemical warfare agents (CWAs) is rarely done with CWAs.
Outside a small number of licensed facilities, sensor arrays, sorbents and
sampling protocols are developed, optimised and published using simulants:
compounds chosen to behave like an agent while being safe enough for an ordinary
laboratory. The choice of simulant therefore sets a ceiling on what a published
detection result can be taken to mean, and that choice is made by a convention
rather than by a measurement.

The dependence is structural, not incidental. The standing reviews of agent
destruction and detection organise a large literature in which reactivity and
sensing chemistry are established on surrogates and extrapolated to agents
\cite{kim2011,jang2015}, and the same pattern runs through the chromogenic,
fluorogenic and supramolecular recognition chemistry developed for nerve agents
\cite{royo2007,sambrook2013,eubanks2007}. Much current field instrumentation
descends from a colorimetric sensor-array lineage established on ordinary
analytes and odours \cite{rakow2000,lin2011,askim2013} and then transferred to
the agent problem, so the compounds on which an array is characterised are
inherited from that lineage rather than fixed by the chemistry. Our own work sits
on both sides, with arrays developed on surrogates \cite{hazref,ondeviceref} and
optical detection closed against authentic Novichok compounds
\cite{group3ref}; the second is rare enough that the first is where most of the
field's evidence is generated.

What a simulant is for differs by subfield, and so does what makes one adequate.
For environmental fate and transport the criterion has been reviewed explicitly
\cite{bartelthunt2008}; for adsorption and catalytic hydrolysis in
metal--organic frameworks it is the simulant that establishes the mechanism and
the kinetics \cite{mondloch2015,moon2015}; in decontamination the
organophosphorus simulant dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP) is the compound
whose removal is quantified \cite{ebeam}. In every case the inference runs from
the simulant to the agent, and the warrant for it is a resemblance argument.

The convention is property matching. A recent critical review of liquid,
low-toxicity simulants states the criterion plainly: align physicochemical
properties such as hydrophobicity, volatility and solubility with those of the
agent, and the simulants it assesses are described as presenting minimal
toxicity risks \cite{webb2025}. That review is confined to liquid-phase
sampling, but the same criterion underwrites simulant choice in vapour-phase
detection, where the quantity of interest is not partitioning but a chemical
response.

Two assumptions are built into this practice, and both are testable. The first
is that resemblance in structural and physicochemical descriptors implies
resemblance in the property a sensor actually reports. For adsorption in
metal--organic frameworks this has been examined directly: simulating four
common simulants and two nerve agents across 2969 frameworks showed that
simulants reproduce agent adsorption behaviour only partially, and that the
agreement depends on which framework and which agent \cite{agrawal2018}. The
analogous question for the electronic properties that govern a chemical response
has not been asked. The second assumption is that a simulant is safe, and it is
usually supported by the absence of an agent-class hazard label rather than by a
toxicity value, although where simulants are handled at scale the protective
equipment is specified against measured permeation and exposure data
\cite{ppesuit}.

Both assumptions can be checked against measurements that already exist. The
electronic structure of small molecules is accessible at semi-empirical
tight-binding cost \cite{gfnxtb2017,bannwarth2019,xtbreview2021}, and acute
inhalation toxicity has been derived by a formal regulatory process for several
hundred chemicals, including the agents themselves \cite{watson2006}. Ranking
compounds along a sparse regulator-derived hazard axis bounded by an explicit
applicability domain is the approach we have applied elsewhere \cite{dbpcarto};
here it is turned on the compounds the detection field uses on itself. We ask
where simulants and agents actually sit relative to
one another, how much of that answer is fixed by the data rather than by the
analyst's choice of compound panel, descriptor space and statistic, whether the
incumbent simulants are in fact safe and whether that claim rests on a value or
on an absence, and whether fidelity and safety are coupled.

The first question turned out to have a different shape than we expected. We
began by comparing descriptor spaces, found a difference, and then found on
audit that it did not survive extending the compound panel, correcting for the
number of statistics computed, or removing a descriptor that was an exact
function of two others in the same space. What the data support is reported
below as such, with the earlier reading alongside it, because the way a nominal
result of this kind arises is part of what a reader of this literature needs.

\paragraph{Dual-use statement.} Throughout, the optimisation direction is fixed:
fidelity to the detection-relevant properties is to be maximised while
phosphorus electrophilicity, acetylcholinesterase reactivity, alkylating
capacity and predicted mammalian toxicity are to be minimised. No analysis here
ranks compounds by potency. The structural filter in Section~\ref{sec:alerts}
excludes candidates and never orders them, and we did not extend the toxicity
model of Supplementary Section~\ref{S-sec:qsar} to the organophosphorus class,
because a model predicting mammalian lethality for structures not yet made would
invert the direction of the work. That a predictive model trained for safety can
be run in reverse is not hypothetical \cite{urbina2022}, and how much of such a
method to disclose is itself a live problem \cite{cbrndisclose}. Mechanistic
modelling of organophosphorus lethality is established and separately published,
including by the present authors \cite{qsarld50}; what is withheld is its use as
a design objective over novel structures, not the literature's existence.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Compound set and the two panels}
\label{meth:panels}
Eleven authentic agents are common to both panels: sarin GB, soman GD, tabun GA,
cyclosarin GF, VX, sulfur mustard HD, lewisite~1 L, hydrogen cyanide AC,
cyanogen chloride CK, phosgene CG and chloropicrin PS. Three compounds are
treated as the incumbent simulants: DMMP, dichloromethane and dipropylene glycol
monomethyl ether (DPGME).

We call these three incumbent rather than designated, because the documentary
record is more specific than either word suggests. Searching for an instrument
that designates them as chemical warfare agent simulants, we found that two of
the three carry a documented designation for a purpose other than the one they
are now used for, and the third carries none.

DMMP was identified as a test simulant for canister and cartridge filtration
against GB and HD in a NIOSH respirator programme \cite{niosh2002simulant}, a
document that also states the simulant information it provides is not to be used
as a recommendation of any simulant, and whose property at issue is adsorptive
breakthrough rather than detection chemistry. DPGME appears as ``a preferred G
agent simulant'' in a patent assigned to the United States Department of the Army
\cite{seitzinger2006simulant}, in a training composition formulated with a
fluorescent dye and justified on ion-mobility detectability, so the application
is field training rather than sensor validation. For dichloromethane we found no
designating document of any kind: it is absent from the NIOSH candidate list and
from the Man-in-Simulant Test programme, and present in this role, as far as we
can establish, only in the research literature.

We state what that search covered, because a negative claim about documents is
only as good as its scope. We searched the standard-setting and test-procedure
literature reachable without a subscription or a clearance: NIOSH and NPPTL
respirator programme documents, US Army and DEVCOM technical reports indexed by
the Defense Technical Information Center, United States patents assigned to the
Department of the Army, National Academies assessments of Department of Defense
test programmes, ASTM and NATO standards catalogues, and the peer-reviewed
simulant literature cited here. Two Test Operations Procedures whose titles bear
on the question (TOP 08-2-196A and TOP 10-2-022B) we could not obtain, and
national CBRN defence manuals are generally not public. A designation in one of
those would change the paragraphs above but not the measurements below.

Two observations follow, and they shape how the rest of this paper should be
read. A designation is specific to a measurement: a compound chosen because it
breaks through a carbon bed like sarin has been validated for adsorption, and
nothing in that transfers to an optical or electrochemical response, while a
compound chosen because it lights up an ion-mobility detector in a training lane
has been validated for that detector and not for a colorimetric array. The
sensor literature using these three is using designations issued for other
purposes. The compounds with the clearest designations, in turn, are not these
three: methyl salicylate is the simulant of the United States Army's
Man-in-Simulant Test programme, chosen for sulfur mustard on 1994 penetration
trials \cite{nrc1997mist}, and the NIOSH candidate list consists of DFP, DIMP,
DEEP, TEP, dibutyl sulfide, CEES, malathion and methyl salicylate. Those
compounds are in the second panel described below, not the first. A recent
survey reaches the general form of the same conclusion, that simulants have been
selected largely on their historical use and that no standard for chemical agent
simulants exists \cite{lawrence2025simulants}.

The two panels differ only in the non-agent pool, and the difference is the
point. The twenty-eight-compound panel adds fourteen interferents and solvents
routinely reported in sensor-array selectivity panels. The
thirty-five-compound panel adds, to those, five further compounds used as
simulants in the sensor, sorbent and decontamination literature (diisopropyl
fluorophosphate DFP, diethyl chlorophosphate DCP, diethyl cyanophosphonate DCNP,
2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide CEES and methyl salicylate), classified as simulants
rather than as interferents, together with two organophosphorus pesticides,
dichlorvos and malathion. We class the pesticides as interferents because the
literature we surveyed does not use them to stand in for an agent, and flag the
decision as contestable: both carry the phosphoryl motif that the structural
filter of Section~\ref{meth:filter} treats as agent-class.
Section~\ref{res:panels} reports how sensitive the result is to exactly this
kind of decision.

The thirty-five-compound panel is the primary one, and the five it adds are the
ones with documentary standing: DFP and methyl salicylate appear in the NIOSH
candidate list quoted above, methyl salicylate is the Man-in-Simulant Test
simulant \cite{nrc1997mist}, and all five are in current use. A comparison asking
whether three compounds are close to the agents while excluding the obvious
alternatives is not a test of those three. We report the twenty-eight-compound
panel throughout with equal numerical detail, because what it shows beside the
larger one is the paper's result, and not to support a conclusion. All class
labels were read from a single canonical table and never assigned by hand.

\subsection{Descriptors}
Structural and physicochemical descriptors (51 after excluding run metadata)
were computed with RDKit \cite{rdkit} from canonical SMILES, and include the
Crippen atomic-contribution estimates of logP and molar refractivity
\cite{wildman1999}, the fragment-based topological polar surface area
\cite{ertl2000} and the $\kappa$ shape indices \cite{kier1985}. Interpretable
descriptors were used throughout in preference to a substructural fingerprint
such as ECFP \cite{rogers2010}, because the quantity of interest here is the
position of a compound along named physical axes rather than a similarity score.
Identifiers, formulae and molecular weights were resolved against PubChem
\cite{pubchem2025}; the EPA CompTox Dashboard \cite{williams2017} was the
intended second route but its programmatic interface was not reachable from
either network used here, so identities rest on PubChem and on the identifiers
printed in the regulatory source documents, and no property value was taken from
a prediction service.

Electronic descriptors were computed with GFN2-xTB
\cite{bannwarth2019}, the second-generation parametrisation of the
geometry--frequency--noncovalent tight-binding family \cite{gfnxtb2017,
xtbreview2021}, in the gas phase; the implicit solvation model available for
these methods \cite{ehlert2021} was not applied, since vapour-phase response is
the property of interest. Conformers were generated with ETKDGv3 \cite{wang2020,riniker2015}, optimised
with MMFF94 \cite{halgren1996} (UFF \cite{rappe1992} for lewisite~1, the only
compound lacking MMFF parameters), then optimised and evaluated at GFN2 level.
Ensembles were enumerated from the distance-geometry stage rather than through a
dedicated sampling workflow \cite{pracht2020}, as these descriptors are not
strongly conformation dependent. Properties for the 28-compound set were
Boltzmann averaged over up to five conformers at 298.15~K with tight
optimisation; the larger AEGL screening set used a single conformer with
\texttt{--opt normal}. On the 28 compounds the two protocols agree to Pearson
$r \ge 0.9857$ across the four electronic descriptors, with mean absolute
differences of 0.029 to 0.052~eV for the orbital quantities and 0.11~D for the
dipole, the largest single dipole discrepancy being 0.95~D at tabun. Which
protocol an analysis uses is fixed by what it compares. The descriptor-space comparison of
Section~\ref{res:panels}, which stays inside the 28 compounds, uses the
Boltzmann-averaged values. The decoupling analysis of Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:h1}
places the 28 and the AEGL pool in one space, so it uses the single-conformer
values for all of them, including the 28; mixing protocols within a distance
calculation would put part of the systematic difference above into the
distances.

Four electronic descriptors are available for every compound: $E_\mathrm{HOMO}$,
$E_\mathrm{LUMO}$, the global electrophilicity index
$\omega=\mu^{2}/2\eta$ in the definition of Parr et al.~\cite{parr1999}, and the dipole
moment. Four further descriptors exist only for the six phosphorus-bearing compounds:
the Mulliken charge at phosphorus $q_\mathrm{P}$, the charge on the phosphoryl
oxygen, the P=O bond order in the Wiberg \cite{wiberg1968} and Mayer
\cite{mayer1983} sense, and the condensed Fukui index $f^{+}(\mathrm{P})$, which
measures the susceptibility of that centre to nucleophilic attack
\cite{parryang1984,yangmortier1986} and whose standing as a reactivity
descriptor is reviewed elsewhere \cite{geerlings2003,domingo2016}. These four
are reported but excluded from any analysis spanning all 28 compounds.

\subsection{Descriptor spaces and distances}
\label{meth:spaces}
Three spaces were compared: structural only, electronic only, and merged.

The electronic space carries three coordinates, not the four that frontier-orbital
descriptor sets of this kind usually report. The fourth, the Parr electrophilicity
index $\omega = \mu^{2}/2\eta$ with $\mu = (E_{\mathrm{HOMO}} +
E_{\mathrm{LUMO}})/2$ and $\eta = (E_{\mathrm{LUMO}} - E_{\mathrm{HOMO}})/2$
\cite{parr1999}, is an exact function of the two orbital energies that accompany
it, so it adds no coordinate. We confirmed this numerically rather than by
inspection: across the panel, $|\omega - \mu^{2}/2\eta|$ reaches at most
$3.6\times10^{-15}$~eV, which is floating-point noise. The correlation filter
described below does not remove it, because $r(E_{\mathrm{LUMO}}, \omega) =
-0.899$ falls below the $|r|>0.95$ threshold; functional dependence and linear
correlation are not the same condition, and a filter built on the second will
pass a variable determined exactly by the first. Three principal components of
the standardised four-variable set account for 97.6\% of its variance, and
removing $\omega$ leaves the rank order of pairwise distances almost unchanged
(Spearman 0.932). We therefore take $E_{\mathrm{HOMO}}$, $E_{\mathrm{LUMO}}$ and
the dipole moment as the electronic space, and report $\omega$ and the condensed
Fukui index as derived quantities in Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:plocal} without admitting
them to any distance. The four reactivity descriptors localised at phosphorus
are undefined for the twenty-two panel compounds that contain none, so they
cannot enter a distance computed over the whole panel either.

Within each space, descriptors with zero variance were removed, then one of each
pair correlated at $|r|>0.95$ was removed with preference for retaining a
protected set (molar refractivity, logP, TPSA, hydrogen-bond acceptor count, four
linear-solvation-energy surrogates and the electronic descriptors). The
four surrogates are linear rescalings of quantities already in the set, namely
the hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, molar refractivity, and the sum of
topological polar surface area and molar refractivity; they are not Abraham
solvation parameters, and three of the four are removed by the correlation
filter at $|r| = 1.00$ in every space. Remaining descriptors were standardised
and distances taken as Euclidean. Two shape indices, $\kappa_2$ and $\kappa_3$,
go negative for the smallest molecules (water, $\kappa_3=-104.0$); repeating
every analysis without them returns the permutation values of
Section~\ref{sec:perm} to three decimal places (structural 0.028, electronic
0.275, merged 0.025, on 36 and 39 descriptors instead of 38 and 41), so no
result below rests on the pathological indices.

\subsection{Nearest-neighbour comparison and permutation test}
\label{sec:perm}
For each authentic agent we recorded the distance to the nearest incumbent
simulant and to the nearest interferent. Because there are fourteen interferents
and three simulants, the minimum of fourteen draws is smaller than the minimum of
three draws by construction, and a paired test of the two distances is
confounded by group size. We therefore enumerated all
ways of designating three of the non-agents as simulants, computed the same
statistic for each, and report the rank of the actual designation as an exact
$p$-value. That is $\binom{24}{3}=2024$ designations on the primary panel and
$\binom{17}{3}=680$ on the twenty-eight-compound panel. The null expectation
for ``an interferent is closer'' follows from the group sizes alone and is
9.63 of 11 on the primary panel and 9.06 on the smaller one.

The hypothesis under test is directional, since property matching is claimed to
place simulants closer to the agents than arbitrary compounds would be. The
reported $p$ is therefore one-sided, the fraction of designations whose mean
$d(\text{nearest simulant}) - d(\text{nearest interferent})$ is at least as
negative as the actual one's. The choice is consequential and we give all three
conventions: scored two-sided as distance from the null mean in either
direction the same statistic gives 0.029, 0.482 and 0.025 for the structural,
electronic and merged spaces, and at twice the smaller tail it gives 0.056,
0.550 and 0.050, so whether the structural value sits inside or outside a
conventional threshold depends on a choice a reader may make differently. This
is the first of the analyst choices catalogued in Section~\ref{res:panels} and
the only one with a defensible default, the hypothesis as its proponents write
it being directional.

\subsection{Match criterion}
\label{meth:match}
Nearest-neighbour rank alone is not sufficient: in a 38-dimensional space a
``nearest'' simulant at a distance of 11.8 is not a match. We therefore
normalised each distance by the median nearest-neighbour distance of the same
space and define a valid match as a normalised distance $\le 2$ together with a
distance smaller than that to every compound outside the designated set. The two
conditions are not equally fragile: the threshold is a convention, the second
condition is not. On the primary panel the incumbent simulants fail the second
condition for all eleven agents, so the count there is zero at every threshold,
including an unbounded one. We report the count at 1.5, 1.75, 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0
in Section~\ref{res:match}, including the one setting at which it changes.

\subsection{Descriptor bootstrap}
\label{meth:dboot}
The conclusions of Section~\ref{res:panels} turn on differences that are small
in absolute terms, 0.12 typical nearest-neighbour separations between DMMP and
diisopropyl fluorophosphate at sarin, and a reader is entitled to ask how much
of that the descriptor choice carries. We resampled the 41 merged-space
descriptors with replacement 1000 times (seed \texttt{0xC0DE}), restandardised,
recomputed the distance matrix and the median nearest-neighbour separation
within each replicate, and recounted the matched agents. Resampling features
rather than compounds is the relevant perturbation, because the compound set is
fixed by the question and the descriptor set is not. The same replicates serve
both simulant definitions, so the difference is paired.

\subsection{Structural filter}
\label{meth:filter}
\label{sec:alerts}
A SMARTS filter was written to exclude candidates carrying agent-class motifs,
following the structural-alert tradition in which a substructure associated with
a mechanism of toxicity serves as a categorical screen \cite{benigni2008,
enoch2011}; the motifs here are agent-class rather than generic and the filter
excludes only. Tier~A covers a phosphoryl group bearing a good leaving group
(fluoride, cyanide, thiolate or activated aryloxy), arsenicals, sulfur and
nitrogen mustard backbones, and cyanide-releasing motifs. Tier~B covers
phosgene-derived acyl halides and chloroformates, halogenated oximes,
$\alpha$-halo carbonyls, and groups making a compound impractical rather than
agent-like (halosilanes, sulfonyl halides, peroxy acids). Tier~C records a
simple phosphoryl ester without a leaving group and is noted rather than
excluded. An earlier version that did not
distinguish leaving groups flagged DMMP at the same tier as sarin and admitted
potassium cyanide to the faithful-and-safe quadrant; both were corrected.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
All analyses are deterministic given the seed and the permutation test is
exhaustive rather than sampled. Numerical work used NumPy 2.5.3 \cite{numpy2020},
SciPy 1.18.1 \cite{scipy2020}, pandas 3.0.5 \cite{mckinney2010} and scikit-learn
1.9.1, figures Matplotlib 3.11.2 \cite{matplotlib2007}, and descriptors RDKit
2026.03.6 \cite{rdkit}.
Code, descriptor tables, parsed toxicity values with their sources and the
figure scripts are in the repository cited under Data and code availability.

\subsection{What the comparison contains decides the answer}
\label{res:panels}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig_panel.png}
\caption{The composition of the comparison, not the fidelity of the compounds,
is what the analysis turns on. (a) For each authentic agent, the distance to the

(diamonds), both as multiples of the typical nearest-neighbour separation of the
merged space on the thirty-five-compound panel. A valid match requires a
normalised distance below the dashed threshold \emph{and} a distance smaller
than that to every compound not designated a simulant, so sarin, soman and
cyclosarin fail on the primary panel despite lying inside the threshold.
(b) Matched agents under 1000 descriptor bootstrap replicates
(Section~\ref{meth:dboot}) for the incumbent three and for the eight compounds
the field uses; circles are means, vertical rules medians, bars 95\% intervals.
(c) Smallest adjusted value over the eighteen tests of each panel configuration
under five ways of accounting for the search (Table~\ref{tab:family}).}
\label{fig:panel}
\end{figure}

On the thirty-five-compound panel, no authentic agent has a valid match to an
incumbent simulant. Figure~\ref{fig:panel}a shows why, and the mechanism is not
the one the earlier version of this work reported. The distances from the agents
to the incumbent simulants hardly move when the panel is extended: sarin sits at
1.14 typical nearest-neighbour separations from DMMP on the smaller panel and
1.19 on the larger, soman at 1.42 and 1.45, cyclosarin at 1.48 and 1.58. The
compounds did not become less faithful. What changed is that diisopropyl
fluorophosphate is present, at 1.07, 0.99 and 1.17 from the same three agents,
and the match criterion requires a simulant to be nearer than every compound not
designated one. The same holds across the panel, with 2-chloroethyl ethyl
sulfide at 0.57 from sulfur mustard where dichloromethane stands at 1.67, and
diethyl cyanophosphonate at 1.80 from tabun where DMMP stands at 2.58.
Admitting the five as simulants gives five matched agents of eleven, against
three on the twenty-eight-compound panel and zero on the primary one. The zero
does not depend on where the threshold was set: no incumbent simulant is the
nearest non-agent compound to any of the eleven agents, so the count stays at
zero however far the threshold is relaxed.

That count is sensitive to the descriptors, and the honest statement of it is
the interval rather than the point. Resampling the 41 merged-space descriptors
with replacement (Section~\ref{meth:dboot}) gives the incumbent three a median
of zero matched agents with a 95\% interval of zero to three, and exactly zero
in 58.8\% of replicates, against a median of five and an interval of four to six
for the eight compounds the field uses. The comparison is what survives: the
difference between the counts is positive in 1000 of 1000 replicates, median
four, interval one to six (Figure~\ref{fig:panel}b). The per-agent reliabilities
in the same replicates separate the firm results from the marginal ones. Sulfur
mustard is matched by 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide in every replicate and tabun by
diethyl cyanophosphonate in 52.5\%, while under the incumbent set alone sarin
recovers DMMP as its nearest compound in 37.8\% of replicates, soman in 14.4\%
and cyclosarin in 12.7\%. The displacement of DMMP at the G agents is therefore
the weakest part of the picture and the sulfur mustard result the strongest,
which reverses what the point estimates alone would suggest.

(45.9\%) for a dimensional rather than a chemical reason, since at equal

Three things hold across the table. On the primary panel all eighteen tests
return $p>0.15$: in no space and under no statistic are the incumbent simulants
distinguishable from compounds drawn at random. Classing the five literature

cannot detect anything on this panel. On the twenty-eight-compound panel, which
differs only in omitting those five and two organophosphorus pesticides, the
incumbents beat random compounds under two of six statistics in the structural
space and three of six in the merged space.

Correcting for the number of times we looked removes almost all of it, and the
exception is instructive. Table~\ref{tab:family} gives the five correction
schemes of Section~\ref{sec:perm}. On the primary panel the question does not
arise, since the smallest nominal value is already 0.151. The schemes matter
only for the twenty-eight-compound panel. There the eighteen-test family gives a
smallest adjusted value of 0.159, correcting the six statistics within each
space gives 0.082, and the Westfall-Young min-$P$ procedure, which uses the
shared permutations and so accounts for the dependence between the tests exactly
rather than approximating it, gives 0.075. That is the closest any correction
brings the panel to the conventional threshold without crossing it. The Li-Ji
approximation it replaces estimates 5.00 effective tests of eighteen and gives

in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reaches 0.05 on any panel under those four schemes.

advance as the single statistic, three tests would have been run and the
twenty-eight-compound structural and merged results would survive at $q=0.042$,

not specify a statistic in advance; we computed six and chose among them after
seeing them, which is the circumstance the correction is for. The comparison is
the paper's own argument made with its own data: one analysis, one data set, five
defensible ways of accounting for the search, and a result significant under one
of them and not the other four. The declaration that would have made the
difference is one nobody in this literature makes, and it has to be made before
the data are seen.

Two further observations bound what the nominal values can mean. The count of
agents for which a compound outside the designated set is nearer than any
incumbent simulant is 11 of 11 in the merged space of the primary panel against
a null expectation of 9.63 set by the 21:3 group-size asymmetry alone, and 6, 10
and 6 of 11 in the three spaces of the smaller panel against 9.06, so the raw
count carries no evidence without the permutation test and in one space runs the
wrong way. On the thirty-five-compound panel with eight simulants, leaving out
one agent at a time moves the structural $p$ between 0.017 and 0.170, the worst
case being removal of tabun; a result that one of eleven agents can carry across
the 0.05 line is not a property of the panel.

\subsection{The difference between the spaces is not dimensional, and the
nominal value is not informative}
\label{res:dim}

The structural space has 38 coordinates and the electronic space has 3, so a
difference between them could be geometric rather than chemical. It is not.
Truncating the standardised structural space to its first three principal
components strengthens the result rather than weakening it, from $p=0.028$ to
$p=0.0044$ on the twenty-eight-compound panel, which leaves the two spaces
62-fold apart in $p$ at matched dimension. The second control is the one that
matters. Of 2000 random three-descriptor subsets of the structural space,
36.3\% reach $p<0.05$, and the observed 38-dimensional value of 0.028 sits at
the 25.8th percentile of that distribution while the electronic value of 0.275
sits at the 77.9th. Both are ordinary draws. The rate itself is inflated by
correlation among the descriptors, falling to 16.9\% on whitened coordinates,
but the argument rests on the percentile and that is unaffected. Both controls
are reported in full in Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:dimdetail}. The spaces
do differ, and the difference is chemical rather than geometric; what the data
do not support is that the difference says anything about the compounds that
were designated.

\subsection{How many agents have a valid simulant depends on the panel}

separation. Sarin (1.13), soman (1.40) and cyclosarin (1.47) are matched, all
three by DMMP; tabun at 2.03 and VX at 3.13 are not. DPGME is nominally VX's

merged space and 3.44 in the structural space that is not a match in any useful
sense. Dichloromethane, used as a pulmonary-agent simulant, is beaten for
phosgene by acetone (3.91) and ethyl acetate (3.94) against its own 6.02 in the
structural and merged spaces, and leads ethyl acetate only in the electronic

The count is stable but not invariant. At thresholds of 1.75 and 2.0 the merged

cyclosarin (1.648) fall outside. The three-agent figure is therefore a statement
about thresholds of 1.75 and above. The electronic space returns four matches at
every threshold tested, the extra one being hydrogen cyanide at 1.335 matched by
dichloromethane, and in no space at any threshold does the count exceed four of
eleven. The headline figure of three refers to the merged space tabulated here,
and the qualification is not cosmetic: the agent the electronic space adds is
matched by dichloromethane rather than by DMMP.

The dependence on the panel is sharper than the dependence on the threshold. On
the thirty-five-compound panel, with the incumbent three still the only
simulants and the five literature compounds in the comparison pool, the merged
space returns no valid match for any of the eleven agents: DMMP loses GB, GD and
GF to diisopropyl fluorophosphate, which is nearer to all three. Admitting the
five as simulants gives five matches of eleven, more than the incumbent set
achieves on either panel, with DFP taking sarin, soman and cyclosarin at 1.07,
0.99 and 1.17, diethyl cyanophosphonate taking tabun at 1.80, and
2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide taking sulfur mustard at 0.57, the closest
agent-simulant pair anywhere in this work. VX remains unmatched in every panel
and space we examined.

We draw no conclusion from this that the field should switch compounds, because
the five were not selected by any procedure we can inspect either, and
Section~\ref{res:panels} applies to them exactly as it applies to the incumbent
three. What the comparison establishes is narrower: the statement ``three of
eleven agents have a valid simulant'' is true of one panel, false of a second,
and replaced by a different list on a third, which is the same instability the
permutation tests report and not an independent finding.

\subsection{What the array actually measures}
\label{res:measured}

Every argument above rests on descriptor distances, and the premise that one of
those spaces corresponds to what a vapour-phase sensor reports is tested in
Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:arraydetail} against measured response from a
29-dye colorimetric array covering twelve of the panel compounds. The test does
not resolve the question in either direction. Of eight panel-channel-space
cells, two reach a nominal $p<0.05$ under Mantel permutation over compounds,
and they favour different descriptor spaces; corrected across the eight, the
smallest adjusted value is 0.098 and no cell reaches $q<0.05$. With six
compounds per plate set the design detects $|\rho| \ge 0.669$ at 80\% power, so
these data exclude a strong association between either descriptor space and the
measured response and nothing weaker. Two artifacts of array analysis of this
kind are reported there because our own first version contained both: dye-axis
centring fixes the between-compound mean cosine at $-1/(n-1)$, which the
observed means clear by only 0.013 to 0.066, so a negative between-compound
similarity is arithmetic rather than evidence; and the measured agent nearest to
DMMP in the ultraviolet channel is tabun rather than sarin, an agent the
descriptor criterion of Section~\ref{res:match} excludes. The claims in the rest
of this paper are written to that limit.

\subsection{The toxicity half of the criterion}
\label{res:toxsummary}

The criterion has a second half: that compounds chosen on physicochemical
grounds present minimal toxicity risk \cite{webb2025}. We tested it on the same
panel and report the analysis in full in the Supplementary Material, since its
conclusions stand on their own data. Three results are needed here.

Dichloromethane has a regulator-derived acute inhalation value and DMMP has
none, EPA having declined to derive an inhalation reference concentration for
want of data (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-meth:aegl}). The two incumbents
therefore differ in how well documented they are and not only in how hazardous,
which matters because DMMP is also the compound against which decontamination
efficiency is quantified \cite{ebeam}. Dichloromethane's value is favourable on
the acute axis, at the 96th percentile of a 207-compound pool, and that ranking
says nothing about the chronic and carcinogenic classification now displacing it
from solvent inventories.

A model cannot fill the gap. A random forest trained on 230 AEGL chemicals
reaches $R^{2}=0.458$ and then predicts every one of twenty held-out military
agents safer than measured, by factors of 2.5 to 2600, while certifying fourteen
of them as inside its applicability domain (Supplementary
Section~\ref{S-sec:qsar}). The failure is not a missing mechanism, since
thirteen training compounds carry an acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting
pharmacophore and one is more acutely toxic than sarin. It is tail regression, a
bias defined in the response space that an input-space domain check cannot see.

Fidelity and acute toxicity are independent across the 207 compounds carrying
both measured axes (Spearman $r=+0.004$, 95\% CI $-0.132$ to $+0.143$), so the
two can be improved separately rather than traded against each other
(Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:h1}). We draw no candidate recommendation from
that: one resting on a single panel and two axes would repeat the error this
paper reports.

\section{Discussion}

The criterion by which low-toxicity simulants are chosen is stated clearly
enough to test \cite{webb2025}: align hydrophobicity, volatility and solubility
with the agent. What the practice does not state is what the chosen compounds
should be compared against, and that decides the answer. On a panel containing
the compounds the field itself uses as simulants, the three incumbents match no
agent and no test falls below $p = 0.15$; on a panel omitting them, three agents
are matched and the smallest nominal value is 0.025.

The mechanism is not a story about fidelity. The incumbents sit almost as close
to the agents on one panel as on the other, moving by 0.03 to 0.10 typical
separations (Section~\ref{res:panels}). What the larger panel supplies is
diisopropyl fluorophosphate, nearer to sarin, soman and cyclosarin than DMMP is,
so a criterion that asks a simulant to beat the alternatives returns nothing.
Resampling the descriptors leaves that comparison intact in every replicate
while showing the individual displacements to differ in firmness: sulfur mustard
is matched by 2-chloroethyl ethyl sulfide in all 1000 replicates, and sarin
recovers DMMP as its nearest compound in 37.8\%. A designation defended by a
comparison that excludes the alternatives is not defended.

An earlier version of this work reported the twenty-eight-compound structural
cell, $p = 0.028$, as the finding and built a thesis on the contrast between it
and the electronic space. Three things undo that reading, and each is a mistake
another group could make the same way. The value does not survive the eighteen
tests of its own family (Benjamini-Hochberg $q = 0.159$), although it would have
survived at $q = 0.042$ had the statistic been fixed in advance, which
demonstrates the paper's argument rather than defending the value. It sits at the
25.8th percentile of 2000 arbitrary three-descriptor slices of the same block.
And it is specific to the panel that omits the five.

Two things we expected to dissolve the contrast between the spaces did not. One
of the four electronic descriptors was the Parr electrophilicity index, an exact
function of two of the others, so the electronic space never had the dimension
we reported; removing it moved the electronic $p$ only from 0.297 to 0.275, so
the descriptor was misdescribed rather than manufacturing the contrast. Matching
the dimensions did not dissolve it either (Section~\ref{res:dim}). The contrast
is real; what fails is the inference from it, because the structural value
anchoring it is an ordinary draw from the subspaces we could have chosen.

What remains is a claim about comparison rather than about chemistry. On the
primary panel we cannot establish that the incumbent simulants are closer to the
agents than compounds drawn at random, in any space, under any of six statistics,
under any of five ways of accounting for the search, and we cannot establish the
opposite either. What we can establish is that the compounds which would make the
comparison informative were not in it.

The documentary record explains how that arose, and it is not a story about
carelessness. Two of the three compounds were designated, and designated
carefully, for measurements that are not the one they are now asked to support
(Section~\ref{meth:panels}): DMMP for adsorptive breakthrough on a filter bed
\cite{niosh2002simulant}, DPGME for ion-mobility detectability in a training
composition carrying a fluorescent dye \cite{seitzinger2006simulant}. Each
selection was fit for the measurement it was made for. The compounds then
carried their status into sensor development, where the governing property is
the response of a dye or an electrode rather than a breakthrough time or an ion
mobility, and nothing in the original validation speaks to that. Dichloromethane
did not make that journey; we found no designating document for it at all.

That mechanism is general. A simulant is validated against a measurement, not
against an agent, and treating the validation as a property of the compound, so
that it travels into a new application, produces a working set whose standing no
descriptor space can reproduce. The corrective is not a replacement compound. It
is to state at the point of use which measurement a simulant was validated
against, and to fix the panel, the space and the statistic before testing a claim
that it is valid for a different one. Agrawal et al.~\cite{agrawal2018} reach the
same place from the other direction: adequacy is a property of the compound
together with the measurement it stands in for, and neither is recoverable from
the compound alone.

Three consequences follow for practice. DMMP is a surrogate for one G agent at a
time rather than for the G series, and not a V-agent surrogate at all: the
descriptor criterion assigns GB, GD and GF to it, while the measured array puts
sarin first in reflectance and tabun, which fails that criterion, first in the
ultraviolet channel (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:arraydetail}), and a
sensor calibrated on DMMP meets a phosphorus centre 2.7 times more electrophilic
than VX presents (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:plocal}). The global
electrophilicity index should not enter a descriptor space, for an arithmetic
rather than a chemical reason: where the frontier orbital energies are present,
$\omega$ adds no information, and a correlation filter will not notice, because
exact functional dependence is compatible with a correlation of $-0.899$. And
the safety of a simulant is a question about evidence before it is one about
hazard, since dichloromethane carries a regulator-derived acute inhalation value
and DMMP carries none (Section~\ref{res:toxsummary}).

We did not attempt to improve the toxicity model by training on organophosphorus
compounds, and the distinction is narrower than a refusal to model the
chemistry. Mechanistic structure--activity models of organophosphorus lethality
exist and we have contributed to them \cite{qsarld50}; such a model is an
instrument for assessing compounds that already exist. What we decline to build
is the same instrument turned into a design objective, a predictor of mammalian
lethality evaluated over structures not yet made inside a loop that proposes
them, which is the configuration in which a safety model becomes a potency
model \cite{urbina2022}. The cost is an acknowledged gap: for the compound class
of greatest interest we can say that a predicted value is unreliable and cannot
supply a better one. Reporting the gap rather than closing it is the correct
outcome, and where to set the boundary between publishing a capability and
withholding it is contested rather than settled \cite{cbrndisclose}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The documentary findings of
Section~\ref{meth:panels} rest on public sources. National CBRN defence manuals
are generally not public, and a designation in one of them would be invisible to
the search we ran; the same applies to two Test Operations Procedures we
identified by title but could not obtain. The claim is therefore that no public
instrument designates these three compounds for the use the sensor literature
makes of them, not that no instrument does. The agent set is eleven compounds
and the nearest-neighbour statistics inherit that: on the larger panel, leaving
out one agent at a time moves the structural $p$ between 0.017 and 0.170. The
permutation test is directional and reported one-sided, with both two-sided
conventions given in Section~\ref{sec:perm}. The two panels are the two we could
build from compounds carrying both descriptor and toxicity data, and a third is
possible, since we class dichlorvos and malathion as interferents although both
carry the phosphoryl motif the structural filter treats as agent-class; a reader
who classed them as simulants would obtain numbers we do not report, so the
three configurations of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} sample the configuration space
rather than census it. The measured-array analysis rests on six or seven
compounds per plate set at four concentrations, so its intervals describe
variation across concentrations within one set rather than agreement between
preparations (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:arraydetail}). GFN2-xTB is
semi-empirical, and the quantities used here are relative within a consistent
protocol rather than absolute.

\section{Conclusions}

Simulant validity is not a property of a compound. It is a property of a
compound set against a comparison, and the practice of choosing chemical warfare
agent simulants does not record what the comparison should contain. On a panel of
thirty-five compounds that includes the five the sensor, sorbent and
decontamination literature also uses as simulants, not one of eleven authentic
agents has a valid match to an incumbent simulant and not one of eighteen tests
falls below $p = 0.15$. The incumbents are no further from the agents on that
panel than on the smaller one; diisopropyl fluorophosphate is simply nearer, and
under descriptor resampling the incumbent three match a median of zero agents
against five for the compounds the field uses, the difference positive in every
one of 1000 replicates. Omitting those five restores three matches and a nominal
structural $p$ of 0.028, a value at the 25.8th percentile of 2000 arbitrary
three-descriptor subspaces of the same block that survives one of five
defensible ways of accounting for the search.

The documentary record gives the reason. DMMP was designated for filtration
breakthrough testing and DPGME for ion-mobility training compositions, each by a
specific instrument and for a measurement that is not detection chemistry; for
dichloromethane we found no designating instrument at all. A simulant is
validated against a measurement rather than against an agent, and that
validation does not travel with the compound into a new application.

The absence of evidence here is not evidence of unsuitability, and we draw no
conclusion that the incumbent simulants are poor choices. What we conclude is
narrower and addressed to method. A simulant claim is testable, this field has
the data to test it, and the test returns whatever the analyst's three undeclared
choices imply. The practical form of that conclusion is a reporting rule: a
simulant result should name the measurement the compound was validated against
and the comparison set it was tested in, and should fix the panel, the descriptor
space and the statistic before the test is run. Until those are declared in
advance, a statement that a compound is a validated simulant reports a convention
rather than a measurement.

Findings from the toxicity axis are in the Supplementary Material and do not
depend on the contested comparison. DMMP has no regulator-derived acute
inhalation value, the agency having declined to derive one, while dichloromethane
has one favourable on the acute axis alone, so the two incumbents differ in how
well documented they are and not only in how hazardous. A random forest trained
on 230 AEGL chemicals predicts all twenty held-out military agents safer than
measured, by factors of 2.5 to 2600, so predicted toxicity cannot stand in for
the missing values (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:qsar}). Fidelity and acute
toxicity are independent across the 207 compounds carrying both axes, and eight
compounds combine top-quartile fidelity with measured safety within one order of
magnitude of dichloromethane (Supplementary Section~\ref{S-res:h1}). We propose
none as a replacement, because proposing one would repeat the error this paper is
about.
