We submit \textit{Dimethyl Methylphosphonate Responds Opposite to Soman, Cyclosarin and
VX in a Colorimetric Array of Authentic Chemical Warfare Agents} for consideration as an Article.

\textbf{What the paper is about, and why we bring it here.} Chemical warfare
agent detection is almost always developed and qualified on simulants, because
the agents themselves are available to few laboratories. Every such
qualification rests on one assumption: that a compound chosen to resemble an
agent on molecular descriptors will resemble it in what the detector measures.
We tested that assumption directly, against colorimetric response measured on
sixteen authentic agents spanning nerve, blood, choking, vesicant and
A-series compounds. This journal sets the standard for whether a detection
claim has been demonstrated against the analyte it names. The question we ask
is one step earlier and bears on every simulant-qualified device in the
literature: whether the surrogate analyte was the right one to begin with.

\textbf{Three counts state the result.} In the three runs we reanalyse, one
designated simulant was measured beside the agents, in one run of one array,
and we found no second co-measurement in the literature we searched. Twelve
exact Mantel tests and six Fisher combinations of them leave no agreement
between descriptor distance and response distance below $q = 0.05$, against a
design that resolves a rank correlation of 0.85 at 80\% power at the corrected
threshold the conclusions use, and 0.67 at a nominal $\alpha$, where the
largest observed is 0.57. On the thirty-five-compound panel, no agent of eleven has a
valid match to an incumbent simulant, at a Benjamini--Hochberg adjusted value of
0.6299, and the compounds that displace the incumbents are themselves
literature-proposed simulants. The array discriminates: in all six
run-channel cells every compound agrees with itself across concentrations more
closely than with the other compounds in that cell. What fails is the transfer
from descriptor space to response space.

\textbf{The specific case will interest sensor developers.} Dimethyl
methylphosphonate is the standard G-agent simulant. Measured beside the agents
in the ultraviolet
channel, its response pattern correlates at $+0.76$ with tabun and $+0.65$ with
sarin but at $-0.49$ with soman, $-0.72$ with cyclosarin and $-0.70$ with VX. A
compound whose measured response is positively correlated with two G agents and
negatively with the other two is not reproducing the series it stands in for.
We draw no conclusion about its suitability for the measurements it was
designated against, respirator filtration and fabric penetration, neither of
which we performed.

\textbf{What this paper is not, stated plainly.} It reports no new sensor, no
demonstration in a complex matrix, and no correlation against an established
analytical method, and we recognise that your guidelines ask for those of a
sensing paper. This is not a sensing paper. It is a measurement-science audit
of the surrogate on which sensing papers are qualified, and the data it audits
could not be produced any other way: the agents cannot be put into a complex
environmental matrix outside a designated laboratory, and the comparison it
makes is between two descriptions of the same compounds rather than between a
sensor and a reference method. We would rather you weigh that against your
scope now than have it surface in review.

\textbf{Why we think it is nonetheless in scope.} Two precedents in
\textit{ACS Sensors} bear on that judgement, and we would rather name them
than leave them to be found. The first is our own. In 2025 this journal
published a bioelectronic nose tested on authentic sarin and VX by a group
that includes two of the present authors, and that paper noted in its
abstract that detection research built on structural simulants may not carry
over to the agents (\textit{ACS Sens.} 2025, 10, 185--195). It asserted the
risk and worked around it. The present manuscript quantifies it, and we have
cited that paper in the Introduction and positioned our contribution against
it explicitly, so that the novelty on offer is the measurement and not the
claim. The second is that this journal publishes work asking whether the
components a sensing paper depends on have in fact been validated, as in the
recent practical guide to choosing and validating aptamers, which opens on a
reproducibility problem in reagents rather than on a device
(\textit{ACS Sens.} 2026, DOI 10.1021/acssensors.6c02357), which the
manuscript cites in its Introduction. Simulants are the
analyte-side equivalent of that problem. Every simulant-qualified detection
claim in the literature rests on an unmeasured transfer, and we think the
readership that builds those detectors is the readership that should see the
measurement.

\textbf{Data provenance.} The colorimetric response data are reanalysed from
three earlier studies by this group, in \textit{Journal of Hazardous
Materials}, \textit{Discover Sensors} and \textit{Sensors and Actuators B},
all cited in the manuscript. Those papers reported classification performance
for three separate agent sets. None of them compared response to molecular
descriptors, none placed the three sets in one analysis, and no figure, table
or passage is reproduced here. The descriptor calculations, the permutation
tests and every result in this manuscript are new.

\textbf{What we propose instead of a replacement compound.} We propose none,
because selecting one on descriptor similarity would repeat the error the
paper is about. What the manuscript specifies is the measurement that would
settle the question: a bridging run in which candidate simulants and the agents
they stand in for are exposed on the same plate, in the same optical channels,
at the same concentrations, within one session. That run yields a response-space
distance between a simulant and its agent measured under identical conditions,
which no amount of descriptor work can supply. It needs no new chemistry and
no new instrument.

\textbf{Length.} The manuscript runs to 9.3 journal pages against the
recommended eight, counted the way the author guidelines specify: 6,622 words
of text, three double-column figures at 500 words each, and 1,137 words of
references. We ask for the extra page for a specific reason. The
paper's claim is that a quantity in common use moves with the conventions used
to compute it, and the only evidence that we did not select a convention for
its answer is the reported sweep. So the manuscript reports six multiplicity
schemes rather than the one that passes, five enumerated analyst freedoms with
the count each produces, both readings of a two-part match criterion where they
disagree, and three statistics we prespecified and then withdrew, with the
reason for each withdrawal. Removing any of that would leave a shorter paper
making a stronger claim than its evidence supports. We have already moved every
reducible block to the Supporting Information, which is where the per-cell
values, the full permutation family, the five freedoms in detail and the two
withdrawn controls now sit; the main text was 10.8 pages before that move. The
Experimental Section is retained in the main article as the guidelines require.

\textbf{Reporting and availability.} This work is a reanalysis of published
tables. We made no measurement and handled no agent. Each of the three source
papers reports that its measurements were made at an Organisation for the
Prohibition of Chemical Weapons designated laboratory under Chemical Weapons
Convention procedures. Analysis code, descriptor tables,
parsed regulatory values with their sources and every figure script are
deposited in a public repository cited in the manuscript, and all permutation
